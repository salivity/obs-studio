How to Find Rendering vs Encoding Lag in OBS Logs

OBS Studio log files contain detailed diagnostic data that clearly separates hardware performance bottlenecks into distinct categories. This guide explains how to locate your OBS log files, identify the specific statistical summaries for streaming or recording sessions, and distinguish between rendering lag (GPU graphics pipeline saturation) and encoding lag (encoder processor overload) so you can apply the correct performance fixes.

Step 1: Exporting the OBS Log File

To analyze a session, you must first generate the log after experiencing the performance issue:

  1. Recreate the lag by running your game and stream/recording for at least 30 to 60 seconds.
  2. Stop the stream or recording.
  3. In the top menu of OBS Studio, click Help > Log Files.
  4. Select View Log Files to open the folder and inspect the latest text file manually, or select Upload Current Log File to quickly view the parsed results in a browser.

Step 2: Locating Session Statistics

Open the log file in any plain text editor (such as Notepad) and scroll to the very bottom, or press Ctrl + F to search backwards from the end of the document. Look for the concluding statistics block of your latest session, typically designated by your output type:

  • For streaming: [rtmp stream: 'adv_stream'] or [rtmp stream: 'simple_stream']
  • For recording: [ffmpeg muxer: 'adv_file_output'] or [ffmpeg muxer: 'simple_file_output']

Directly below these markers, OBS prints summary lines outlining total frames processed and any dropped frames encountered during that specific run.

Step 3: Differentiating Rendering Lag from Encoding Lag

Within the summary block, look for two distinct lines:

Rendering Lag (GPU Compositing Issues)

Look for the line: Number of frames dropped due to rendering lag: X (Y%)

  • What it means: Rendering lag occurs when your graphics card is maxed out rendering 3D graphics (typically your game) and does not have enough compute resources left to composite OBS sources, filters, and scenes. This happens before the frame ever reaches the encoder.
  • Acceptable threshold: Under 1%. Any value above 1% results in visible visual stuttering.
  • Resolution: Cap your in-game frame rate to match your monitor's refresh rate, lower in-game graphics settings, run OBS as an Administrator to ensure Windows prioritizes OBS GPU access, or disable the OBS Studio preview window.

Encoding Lag (Encoder Overload Issues)

Look for the line: Number of frames dropped due to encoding lag: X (Y%)

  • What it means: Encoding lag occurs after OBS successfully composites the scene, but the selected encoder (such as NVENC, AMD VCE/AMF, Intel QuickSync, or x264 CPU encoding) cannot process frames as fast as they are being supplied.
  • Acceptable threshold: Under 1%.
  • Resolution: If using hardware encoding (e.g., NVENC), lower your encoder preset from "Max Quality" or "P7" to "Quality" or "P5", and turn off features like Look-ahead or Psycho Visual Tuning. If using software encoding (x264), adjust your CPU usage preset to a faster option (e.g., from "Medium" to "Fast" or "Veryfast"), or downscale your output resolution (e.g., 1440p to 1080p).

Step 4: Rule Out Network Frame Drops

Do not confuse rendering or encoding lag with network drops. If your log shows: Number of dropped frames due to insufficient bandwidth/connection stall: X (Y%)

Your hardware is performing adequately, but your upload speed, ingest server connection, or local network stability is failing to transmit the video packets. Hardware settings do not need adjustment in this case; instead, lower your video bitrate or switch to a more stable connection.
