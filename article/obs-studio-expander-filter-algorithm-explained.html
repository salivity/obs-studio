OBS Studio Expander Filter Algorithm Explained

The native Expander filter in OBS Studio is built on a standard digital feedforward dynamic range architecture that lowers the volume of audio signals falling below a user-defined threshold. This article breaks down the mathematical and digital signal processing (DSP) mechanics powering the filter, including its level detection methods (Peak and RMS), logarithmic gain computation, and recursive attack/release envelope ballistics.

Feedforward Dynamic Range Architecture

OBS Studio implements downward dynamic range expansion using a classic feedforward digital dynamic range controller (DRC) design. In this topology, the incoming audio signal is split into two paths:

  1. The Sidechain/Detector Path: The signal level is measured, evaluated against threshold and ratio parameters, and used to calculate a smooth gain reduction envelope.
  2. The Direct Signal Path: The uncorrupted original audio is delayed by any internal buffer requirements and then multiplied by the computed linear gain factor.

Because the gain control is derived independently from the output, the filter maintains deterministic, stable behavior across varied hardware configurations without feedback-induced oscillations.

Level Detection: Peak vs. RMS

Before determining how much attenuation to apply, the algorithm measures the signal level through one of two selectable detection algorithms:

  • Peak Detection: The algorithm tracks the instantaneous absolute magnitude of incoming samples (\(|x[n]|\)). Peak detection provides near-instantaneous response to transient audio events, making it ideal for sharp noise gating.
  • RMS (Root-Mean-Square) Detection: The detector calculates the effective energy over time using a continuous running average of squared sample values (\(x[n]^2\)) passed through a first-order leaky integrator. This approximates human loudness perception and results in smoother, more transparent expansion.

Both detection methods convert the measured linear amplitude into the logarithmic decibel (dBFS) scale via standard conversion (\(20 \log_{10}(level)\)) to align with human hearing dynamics.

The Logarithmic Gain Computer

Once the signal level (\(X_{dB}\)) is known, the downward expansion function calculates the target gain reduction. Unlike an upward expander that boosts loud signals, OBS Studio's filter acts purely downward:

  • Above Threshold (\(X_{dB} \ge Threshold_{dB}\)): The signal is left unaffected with a gain factor of \(0\text{ dB}\) (unity gain).
  • Below Threshold (\(X_{dB} < Threshold_{dB}\)): Gain reduction is applied proportionally to the user-selected ratio (\(R:1\)):

\[\Delta G_{dB} = (Threshold_{dB} - X_{dB}) \times (1 - R)\]

When the ratio is set to high values (such as \(10:1\) or higher), the gain computer functions virtually as a noise gate. At lower ratios (such as \(2:1\) or \(4:1\)), it gently lowers the noise floor without creating jarring cutoffs.

Ballistics and Smoothing Envelope

Sudden changes in gain produce audible distortion, clicks, and zipper noise. To prevent this, the raw gain values from the computer are processed through an envelope follower governed by user-defined Attack and Release time constants.

OBS Studio applies first-order Infinite Impulse Response (IIR) low-pass filter ballistics:

\[y[n] = \alpha \cdot y[n-1] + (1 - \alpha) \cdot x[n]\]

The coefficient \(\alpha\) is derived from the sampling rate (\(f_s\)) and the desired time constant (\(\tau\)) in seconds:

\[\alpha = e^{-\frac{1}{\tau \cdot f_s}}\]

  • Attack Time: Dictates how quickly attenuation is removed when signal levels rise back above the threshold (restoring unity gain).
  • Release Time: Dictates how gradually gain reduction is introduced when the signal falls below the threshold, ensuring speech tails and natural room reverberation decay organically.

Linear Conversion and Application

In the final stage, the smoothed decibel gain value is transformed back to a linear multiplier:

\[g_{linear} = 10^{\frac{G_{dB}}{20}}\]

The linear factor is multiplied against the delayed input samples buffer across all audio channels. The result is transparent attenuation of ambient room noise, keyboard clicks, and HVAC hum whenever active speech ceases.
