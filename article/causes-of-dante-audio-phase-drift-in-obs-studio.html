Causes of Dante Audio Phase Drift in OBS Studio

Audio phase drift when routing Dante Virtual Soundcard (DVS) feeds across busy corporate networks into OBS Studio degrades audio quality, causing comb filtering, hollow sound, and sync loss. This issue stems from a combination of network packet jitter, clock synchronization limitations inherent to software-based audio endpoints, operating system audio buffering, and OBS Studio's internal audio clock management. Understanding how these network and software elements interact is essential for diagnosing and resolving phase discrepancies in multi-channel or multi-source environments.

1. IEEE 1588 PTP Jitter and Network Congestion

Dante relies on the Precision Time Protocol (PTPv1 / IEEE 1588-2002) to synchronize audio samples across a network within sub-microsecond accuracy. On a busy corporate network lacking strict Quality of Service (QoS) configurations, high-bandwidth data transfers, VoIP, and general network traffic compete directly with Dante's synchronization and media streams.

When switches do not prioritize Dante clock packets (typically DSCP 56) and audio packets (typically DSCP 46), packet arrival times become inconsistent—a phenomenon known as network jitter. While hardware Dante devices contain dedicated hardware chips to handle this jitter, software solutions are far more vulnerable to it.

2. DVS Clocking and Software Host Limitations

Unlike dedicated hardware endpoints equipped with Dante Brooklyn or Ultimo chipsets, Dante Virtual Soundcard is a purely software-based implementation. It relies on the host computer's operating system and internal CPU clock to maintain timing.

Because standard PC motherboards utilize unmanaged, crystal oscillators that are sensitive to temperature and load fluctuations, DVS inherently suffers from minor clock drift compared to hardware grandmaster clocks. When a congested network delays PTP packets, DVS cannot dynamically adjust its software clock accurately, causing the audio stream to drift out of phase relative to other networked sources or local inputs.

3. Buffer Overruns and Underruns (Sample Dropping)

DVS provides selectable latency buffers (such as 4ms, 10ms, or 20ms) to counteract network jitter. If network traffic causes packet delivery times to exceed the configured DVS buffer window, the audio engine encounters an underrun or overrun.

To prevent an audible click, pop, or complete stream failure, audio drivers often drop or insert micro-samples. In a multi-channel setup—or when comparing a Dante audio stream to a locally captured video or audio feed—these missing or duplicated samples permanently shift the relative timing of the channels, resulting in noticeable phase drift over time.

4. Driver Architecture: ASIO vs. WDM/WASAPI

The method OBS Studio uses to ingest audio from DVS plays a critical role in phase stability:

  • WDM / WASAPI: Ingesting DVS via standard Windows Audio drivers forces the audio stream through the Windows Audio Engine. This introduces variable resampling, shared mode buffering, and system-level latency variations that directly cause phase skew between channels.
  • OBS-ASIO: Utilizing a dedicated ASIO input plugin avoids the OS mixer, delivering the DVS stream directly to OBS. If OBS is not configured to use ASIO for DVS ingest, phase drift across multi-channel inputs is virtually guaranteed over extended periods.

5. OBS Studio Internal Resampling and Drift Compensation

OBS Studio maintains its own internal audio clock to align audio tracks with incoming video frames. If the DVS audio sample rate (e.g., 48 kHz) does not match the OBS canvas audio sample rate exactly, OBS applies real-time software resampling.

Furthermore, if OBS detects that an audio feed is falling behind the system clock or the video frame rate, its internal clock drift compensation logic dynamically adjusts audio playback by subtly dropping or adding audio samples. When ingesting stereo or multi-mic arrays over separate DVS streams, OBS may adjust these streams independently, pulling them out of phase with each other.
