How OBS Studio Handles Mobile VFR Camera Feeds

This article explains how OBS Studio ingests and processes variable frame rate (VFR) feeds from mobile broadcasting applications without dropping frames. Mobile devices frequently alter frame rates dynamically due to thermal throttling, fluctuating lighting, and wireless network instability. To maintain a smooth broadcast, OBS Studio uses an internal constant frame rate architecture, asynchronous source buffering, timestamp translation, and smart frame duplication.

The Conflict Between Mobile VFR and Broadcast CFR

Smartphones running mobile broadcast tools (such as SRT, RTMP, NDI, or WebRTC applications) adjust their capture rates on the fly. A device targeting 60 FPS may drop to 42 FPS in low light or down to 28 FPS when processor thermal limits are reached.

Standard streaming destinations like YouTube, Twitch, and linear broadcast systems require a strict Constant Frame Rate (CFR). OBS Studio bridges this gap by decoupling the capture rate of the incoming mobile feed from the canvas render rate, preventing the encoder from skipping frames or producing broken video streams.

Asynchronous Threading and the Canvas Render Loop

OBS Studio operates on an independent rendering loop tied directly to the user’s configured output frame rate (e.g., 60 FPS). This internal render tick runs on its own dedicated graphics thread and does not wait for incoming video sources to deliver a frame.

When a mobile app sends video:

  1. The incoming stream is received over the network and decoded by the corresponding input module (such as the Media Source, SRT receiver, or NDI plugin).
  2. The decoded video frame is pushed into a shared memory buffer on the GPU as a texture.
  3. When the canvas render loop ticks, it fetches the most recent frame available in the buffer.

Because the rendering loop pulls frames rather than having frames pushed onto it directly, a delay or variation in the mobile device's capture rate never stalls the compositing engine.

Timestamp Resampling and Frame Duplication

To prevent frame drops when a mobile feed falls below the project's target frame rate, OBS relies on Presentation Timestamps (PTS) and frame holding:

  • Frame Duplication (Padding): If a mobile device drops from 60 FPS to 30 FPS, the source buffer does not receive new frames quickly enough to satisfy every 60 FPS canvas tick. Instead of dropping the frame or rendering a blank space, OBS re-renders the previous texture. By duplicating the last available frame until a new one arrives, OBS smoothly pads the stream into a valid CFR signal.
  • Timestamp Normalization: OBS normalizes the source's native timestamps against its own internal master clock. Even if the mobile feed delivers frames at irregular intervals (e.g., 16ms, then 33ms, then 22ms), OBS maps them to the nearest valid canvas tick, maintaining visual continuity.

Network Jitter Buffering

VFR feeds over mobile connections are often accompanied by packet jitter. To avoid frame drops caused by out-of-order or late network packets, OBS input protocols deploy jitter buffers:

  • SRT/RTMP Ingestion: Protocols like SRT buffer packets for a defined latency window (e.g., 200ms to 1000ms). This gives late frames time to arrive and undergo sequential decoding.
  • WebRTC/VDO.Ninja Integration: Browser-based inputs adjust their internal receive buffers dynamically, absorbing the bursty transmission common to cellular networks before the frames enter OBS's render pipeline.

Audio-Video Synchronization

A common side effect of VFR processing is audio desynchronization, as audio is natively continuous while video is sampled intermittently. OBS handles this through its internal sync engine:

  1. Audio samples are strictly tied to high-precision system timecodes.
  2. If video frames arrive late or with uneven spacing, the video frame is displayed at the exact time matching its corresponding audio timestamp.
  3. If network lag causes the incoming video buffer to underrun completely, OBS maintains the audio timeline and holds the last good video frame, resynchronizing seamlessly as soon as the mobile application resumes frame delivery.
