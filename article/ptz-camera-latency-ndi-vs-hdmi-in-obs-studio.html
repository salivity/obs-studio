PTZ Camera Latency: NDI vs HDMI in OBS Studio

When integrating Pan-Tilt-Zoom (PTZ) cameras into OBS Studio, latency is often the deciding factor between a smooth, synchronized production and a frustrating broadcast setup. While both HDMI capture cards and NDI protocols are industry standards for feeding PTZ video into OBS, they handle video processing, encoding, and transport very differently. This guide breaks down the latency differences between NDI and HDMI capture, explains the technical reasons behind these delays, and highlights the practical impact on your OBS Studio workflow.

Latency Performance: HDMI Capture Cards

Direct HDMI capture via a PCIe capture card or USB 3.0 capture device delivers the lowest latency available for PTZ cameras in OBS Studio.

  • Typical Latency: 30 to 80 milliseconds (roughly 1 to 5 frames at 60 fps).
  • Signal Path: PTZ sensor → uncompressed HDMI output → physical capture card hardware → OBS Studio canvas.
  • Why It Is Fast: HDMI transmits uncompressed raw video data. High-quality PCIe cards (such as those from Blackmagic Design, Magewell, or Elgato) transfer this data directly to system memory with negligible processing delay. USB 3.0 capture dongles add only a marginal buffer (typically 10–20ms higher than PCIe).

Because the signal is uncompressed, there are no encoding or decoding steps to delay the feed.

Latency Performance: NDI (Network Device Interface)

NDI transmits video, audio, and control data over a standard local area network (Ethernet). Latency here depends heavily on whether your PTZ camera uses Full NDI (High Bandwidth) or NDI|HX.

  • Full NDI Latency: 50 to 120 milliseconds. Full NDI uses lightweight, intra-frame compression (around 100–150 Mbps for 1080p60). The compression and decompression process is fast, resulting in a delay that closely rivals HDMI.
  • NDI|HX (HX2 / HX3) Latency: 100 to 250+ milliseconds. Most cost-effective PTZ cameras use NDI|HX, which relies on H.264 or H.265 (HEVC) compression to keep bandwidth low (typically 10–30 Mbps). The inter-frame encoding inside the camera and the decoding process within the OBS NDI plugin introduce measurable lag. NDI|HX3 bridges this gap somewhat, reducing latency to roughly 80–120ms if supported by both the camera and the network environment.

Key Factors Causing the Latency Gap

  1. Compression Overhead: HDMI requires zero compression. NDI requires video to be compressed at the camera level and decompressed inside OBS via the CPU or GPU.
  2. Network Jitter and Buffering: NDI relies on network infrastructure. Network switches, bandwidth saturation, and OBS NDI plugin frame buffering introduce variable latency to prevent frame drops.
  3. Internal Camera Processing: Many PTZ cameras prioritize network encoding efficiency over speed when using NDI|HX, adding processing frames before the video packet even leaves the camera’s Ethernet port.

Practical Impact in OBS Studio

  • Audio Synchronization: With HDMI, audio introduced via a separate USB interface or mixer often requires little to no delay filter in OBS (usually 0 to 50ms). With NDI, especially NDI|HX, you will almost always need to apply an audio sync offset of 100ms to 250ms in OBS's Advanced Audio Properties to prevent lip-sync mismatches.
  • IMAG (Image Magnification): If you are projecting the camera feed onto live screens in the same room as the speaker, HDMI is required. Any latency exceeding 100ms creates an unnatural, distracting echo effect between the real speaker and the screen. Full NDI is borderline for IMAG, while NDI|HX is too slow.
  • PTZ Controller Responsiveness: Pan, tilt, and zoom movements feel immediate over HDMI. Over NDI, operator joystick inputs will exhibit a slight visual delay on the OBS preview screen, requiring the camera operator to anticipate stops to avoid overshooting shots.

HDMI capture cards provide the lowest and most consistent latency for time-sensitive productions, while NDI trades a small amount of speed for the immense convenience of sending video, power (PoE), and PTZ control over a single Ethernet cable.
