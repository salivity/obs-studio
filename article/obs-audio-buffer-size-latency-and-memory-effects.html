OBS Audio Buffer Size: Latency and Memory Effects

Adjusting the audio buffering time in OBS Studio's advanced settings directly impacts your stream's responsiveness and stability. Increasing this value gives your system a safety cushion to process sound without glitching, but it fundamentally introduces higher audio delay and slightly alters RAM usage. This article breaks down the precise latency and memory consequences of increasing the audio buffer size, helping you choose the right balance for your streaming or recording setup.

Understanding Audio Buffering in OBS

In OBS Studio, the audio buffer is a temporary holding area in system memory (RAM) where incoming audio samples are stored before being processed, encoded, and multiplexed with video. This setting is found under Settings > Advanced > Audio > Audio Buffering Time. The default value is typically set to a low duration (such as 30ms to 50ms) to maintain near-instantaneous processing.

Latency Consequences

Latency is the most significant and immediate tradeoff when expanding the audio buffer size:

  • Increased Audio Processing Delay: Increasing the buffer size from 50ms to 200ms adds an explicit 150ms delay before that audio is passed through the encoding pipeline.
  • Audio-to-Video Desynchronization: If audio buffering increases while video buffering remains constant, sound can become out of sync with your camera and gameplay. While OBS attempts to compensate internally, extreme values often require manually adjusting the Sync Offset (ms) in the Advanced Audio Properties for individual sources.
  • Delayed Audio Monitoring: If you monitor your microphone or desktop audio through headphones via OBS, a larger buffer creates a noticeable "echo" effect in your ears. Delays above 30–50ms become disorienting for live commentary and singing.

Memory (RAM) Consequences

While increasing the buffer inherently demands more storage, the impact on modern PC hardware is negligible:

  • Minimal Memory Footprint: Audio data—even uncompressed 32-bit float stereo at 48kHz—requires relatively little memory. One second of stereo uncompressed audio consumes roughly 384 kilobytes (KB) of RAM. Increasing the buffer from 50ms to 500ms uses only fractions of a megabyte of extra memory across all active audio tracks.
  • Stability vs. Overhead: The memory cost is so low that even budget systems with 8GB of RAM will not experience performance degradation from memory consumption alone. The actual load remains tied to CPU scheduling, not memory starvation.

When Should You Increase the Buffer?

  • Eliminating Audio Crackle and Dropouts: If your CPU experiences brief 100% utilization spikes, the audio thread can be momentarily starved, causing pops, clicks, or dropped audio packets. A larger buffer (e.g., 80ms to 150ms) gives the processor extra headroom to recover without disrupting the audio stream.
  • Complex Multi-Track Routing: If you are using heavy digital signal processing (DSP) filters, extensive VST plugins, or complex virtual audio routing (such as Voicemeeter or multiple virtual cables), a larger buffer stabilizes the signal chain.

Recommended Balance

For most content creators, the default setting is optimal. Only increase the buffer in small increments (such as 20ms at a time) if you consistently hear audio stuttering under heavy system loads. If low latency and live audio monitoring are priorities, keep the buffer as low as your CPU can reliably maintain without causing audio degradation.
