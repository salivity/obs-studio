OBS Studio Gain Staging for Line-Level Mixers

This article explains how OBS Studio processes and manages audio gain staging when ingesting professional +4 dBu line-level signals from broadcast mixing desks. It outlines the transition from professional analog reference levels to the digital domain, details OBS Studio's internal 32-bit floating-point audio processing, and provides clear steps to establish proper headroom, avoid digital clipping, and ensure broadcast-compliant streaming output.

The Analog-to-Digital Transition (+4 dBu to dBFS)

Professional broadcast mixing desks operate at a nominal analog standard of +4 dBu (typically reading 0 VU on analog meters). OBS Studio, however, operates strictly in the digital domain where signal levels are measured in decibels relative to full scale (dBFS), where 0 dBFS represents the absolute digital ceiling.

OBS Studio itself does not contain analog hardware; it relies entirely on the audio interface, capture card, or digital network protocol (such as Dante via virtual soundcards) converting the signal. To preserve gain staging:

  • Calibration Standards: Professional converters typically align +4 dBu (0 VU) to either -18 dBFS (EBU standard) or -20 dBFS (SMPTE standard).
  • Preventing Converter Clipping: If an analog desk pushes peaks up to +24 dBu, an interface calibrated to -20 dBFS headroom will hit +4 dBFS on the analog side relative to full scale, resulting in irreversible converter clipping before the signal ever reaches OBS. Ensure the hardware interface has sufficient input pad or headroom to absorb the desk's maximum output without clipping.

OBS Studio Internal Audio Engine Processing

Once the audio passes the Analog-to-Digital Converter (ADC) into the operating system driver, OBS ingests the stream into its internal audio pipeline.

  • 32-Bit Float Architecture: OBS Studio processes audio internally using 32-bit floating-point precision. Within the OBS mixing environment, audio tracks theoretically have virtually unlimited dynamic range above 0 dBFS, meaning internal operations (such as summing multiple sources or applying EQ) will not clip mathematically between processes.
  • The Input Limitation: Floating-point headroom applies only after ingestion. If the incoming hardware driver clips at standard 24-bit or 16-bit integer depths (0 dBFS), the incoming audio is already distorted. Lowering the OBS fader cannot repair a signal that clipped at the audio interface level.

Digital Faders and Gain Scaling

The faders in the OBS Studio Audio Mixer act as digital multipliers applied to the incoming data stream:

  • 0.0 dB Fader Setting: Leaves the digital stream from the audio driver bit-transparent (unity gain). For broadcast desks delivering a fully pre-mixed feed, the OBS fader should remain locked at 0.0 dB.
  • Fader Color Zones: OBS meters provide visual feedback in dBFS:
    • Green (-inf to -20 dBFS): Safe baseline noise and low-level signal.
    • Yellow (-20 to -9 dBFS): Target zone for nominal broadcast dialogue and program content.
    • Red (-9 to 0 dBFS): Peak zone; signals approaching -3 to 0 dBFS risk clipping final lossy broadcast encoders (such as AAC or Opus).

Managing Output and Encoder Headroom

While OBS avoids internal clipping via floating-point mathematics, the final destination—the stream or recording encoder—outputs to fixed integer formats (such as 16-bit PCM or lossy AAC).

  1. Unity Pass-Through: Manage all dynamic control, leveling, and ducking at the broadcast console rather than inside OBS. The broadcast console's master output meter should reflect the target program level.
  2. Target Levels: Ensure average dialogue settles around -18 to -14 dBFS on the OBS meters, with transients never exceeding -2 dBFS.
  3. True Peak Limiting: Lossy compression algorithms used in live streaming (such as AAC) can cause inter-sample peaks that generate audible distortion if the pre-encoded signal sits too close to 0 dBFS. Add an OBS Limiter filter on the master output with a threshold set to -2.0 dB with a fast release (around 60 ms) strictly as a brickwall safety measure, ensuring the broadcast console retains primary control over the mix dynamics.
