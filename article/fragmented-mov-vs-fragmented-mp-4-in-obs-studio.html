Fragmented MOV vs Fragmented MP4 in OBS Studio

When recording mission-critical footage in OBS Studio, choosing between Fragmented MOV (fMOV) and Fragmented MP4 (fMP4) protects against data loss caused by system crashes, power cuts, or blue screens. While standard MP4 and MOV files corrupt completely if not finalized properly, both fragmented formats write video in discrete, self-contained segments that remain readable up to the exact second of an interruption. The trade-offs between fMOV and fMP4 do not concern crash resilience, but rather center on post-production compatibility, multi-track audio support, operating system integration, and workflow efficiency.

Crash Resilience: A Common Advantage

Traditional MP4 and MOV containers store a crucial indexing header, called the "moov atom," which is written only after the recording is manually stopped. If OBS Studio crashes mid-session, that index is never written, rendering the entire file unreadable.

Both fragmented MOV and fragmented MP4 resolve this by splitting the recording into a continuous sequence of short fragments consisting of a movie fragment header (moof) followed by media data (mdat). Because each chunk contains its own index, a sudden termination preserves all footage recorded up to that final chunk. In terms of fault tolerance and data recovery, fMOV and fMP4 perform identically.

Non-Linear Editor (NLE) Compatibility

The primary trade-off between the two formats lies in how editing software handles fragmented wrappers:

  • Fragmented MOV: Highly compatible with professional editing workflows. Software such as DaVinci Resolve, Adobe Premiere Pro, and Apple Final Cut Pro historically handle QuickTime-based wrappers with high stability. Apple ecosystem editors often process fragmented MOV files directly without requiring a container rebuild.
  • Fragmented MP4: While standard MP4 is the universal standard for video playback, certain video editors still struggle to parse the multi-fragment structure of fMP4 correctly. Users may encounter timeline indexing lag, dropped frames during scrubbing, or audio-video desynchronization in older or less forgiving NLE versions. In these scenarios, OBS Studio's built-in "Remux Recordings" tool must be used to convert the fMP4 into a standard MP4 before editing.

Audio Track Handling and Codec Flexibility

Mission-critical productions typically capture separated audio tracks for microphones, game audio, communications, and system sounds:

  • Fragmented MOV: QuickTime containers offer native support for uncompressed Linear PCM (LPCM) audio alongside standard AAC. For professional production where high dynamic range, uncompressed multi-track audio is mandatory, fMOV paired with PCM audio is the industry standard.
  • Fragmented MP4: The MP4 container primarily utilizes AAC or Opus audio. While efficient and standard for streaming delivery, MP4 containers have stricter constraints regarding uncompressed audio formats and multi-channel configurations. While OBS Studio manages multiple AAC tracks in fMP4 reliably, managing high-fidelity uncompressed tracks is structurally better suited to fMOV.

Native OS Playback and Quick Turnaround

If footage must be immediately reviewed or shared without running it through an editor:

  • Fragmented MP4: Holds a clear advantage on Windows and web platforms. The default Windows Media Player, web browsers, and cloud storage previews (such as Google Drive or Dropbox) read MP4 containers natively.
  • Fragmented MOV: macOS handles MOV natively via QuickTime Player, but default Windows installations often lack the modern decoders to open fragmented MOV containers smoothly without third-party software like VLC Media Player.

Summary: Choosing the Right Format

  • Choose Fragmented MOV if you are on macOS, edit in Final Cut Pro or DaVinci Resolve, require multi-track uncompressed PCM audio, or intend to drop raw recordings straight into a professional post-production timeline without remuxing.
  • Choose Fragmented MP4 if your delivery target is Windows-centric, if you require immediate native previewing across standard media players and cloud platforms, or if your pipeline already relies on universal MP4 delivery and standard AAC audio tracks.
