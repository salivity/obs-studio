How OBS Handles USB Audio Clock Drift

When using multiple asynchronous USB audio interfaces in OBS Studio, independent hardware clock oscillators inevitably cause audio streams to drift over time. This article breaks down how the OBS Studio audio engine detects these discrepancies, relies on operating system timestamps, and employs dynamic resampling and buffer management to maintain audio synchronization without audible artifacts or cumulative latency.

The Problem of Clock Drift in Asynchronous USB Devices

Every USB audio interface contains its own internal crystal oscillator that dictates its physical sampling rate. While two devices may both be set to 48,000 Hz, manufacturing tolerances mean that Device A might produce 48,004 samples per second, while Device B produces 47,996 samples per second. Over the course of a multi-hour stream or recording, this discrepancy causes buffers to either starve (buffer underflow) or fill up faster than they are consumed (buffer overflow), leading to audio dropouts, distortion, and gradual loss of synchronization with video.

High-Resolution Timestamps as the Reference Point

OBS Studio maintains a unified master clock derived from the host system’s high-resolution monotonic timer. When audio samples arrive from distinct capture APIs—such as WASAPI on Windows, CoreAudio on macOS, or ALSA/PulseAudio/PipeWire on Linux—the drivers tag each buffer with a capture timestamp.

Rather than blindly trusting the nominal sample rate of incoming data, the OBS audio engine (libobs/audio-io.c) measures the rate at which samples arrive against the system reference timeline. By mapping hardware presentation timestamps to the engine's timeline, OBS can accurately quantify the precise rate of drift occurring on each individual audio input.

Dynamic Resampling and Rate Correction

To correct drift continuously without introducing audible clicks or pitch warping, OBS Studio utilizes dynamic resampling:

  1. Buffer Level Monitoring: OBS tracks the state of the input FIFO (First In, First Out) ring buffers for each device.
  2. Dynamic Pitch/Rate Adjustment: When the audio engine detects that an interface is consistently delivering more or fewer samples than expected relative to the master clock, it routes the incoming PCM audio through an internal resampler (typically built on libswresample or custom interpolation filters).
  3. Micro-Adjustments: The resampler subtly alters the effective sample rate in fractional increments. If a device is running slightly too fast, the stream is imperceptibly downsampled; if it runs too slow, it is upsampled. This dynamic scaling prevents buffers from reaching capacity limits without creating noticeable pitch shifts.

Buffer Management and Drop/Duplication Fallbacks

If clock drift spikes unexpectedly due to USB bus congestion, high DPC latency, or driver interruptions, dynamic resampling alone may not be enough to prevent an overflow or underflow. In these edge cases, OBS Studio’s audio pipeline implements targeted buffer corrections:

  • Sample Dropping: If a buffer overflows beyond an acceptable threshold, OBS drops the oldest non-processed audio samples to immediately realign the stream to the current presentation timestamp, sacrificing a fraction of a millisecond of audio to preserve long-term sync.
  • Sample Padding: If an underflow occurs, OBS inserts silence or duplicates the boundary sample briefly to avoid stalling the master mix engine.

Integration with the Video Pipeline

All audio streams inside OBS are converted into floating-point planar PCM data and synchronized in uniform frames (typically 10 milliseconds or 1024 samples, depending on output configuration). Because video frames arrive at fixed intervals governed by the video output pipeline, the synchronized audio tracks are interleaved alongside matching video presentation timestamps (PTS). This shared time base ensures that all asynchronous USB devices remain locked not only to one another, but also to the video capture sources.
