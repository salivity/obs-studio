Why Rotated Overlays Look Jagged in OBS Studio

When creators apply aggressive rotation angles to high-contrast graphic overlays in OBS Studio, the edges often display prominent stair-stepping artifacts, commonly known as aliasing or "jaggies." This visual degradation occurs due to a combination of GPU rasterization mechanics, inadequate texture filtering, and the lack of native multi-sample anti-aliasing (MSAA) on OBS scene elements. Understanding these technical limitations allows you to address the root causes and restore smooth edges to your broadcast graphics.

The Absence of Native Geometric Anti-Aliasing

OBS Studio renders its scene graph using Direct3D 11 (Windows) or Metal/OpenGL (macOS/Linux). Each source inside a scene is treated as a flat rectangular polygon (a quad). When you rotate a source at an aggressive angle, the edges of this quad no longer align with the pixel grid of your canvas.

Standard 3D engines use Multi-Sample Anti-Aliasing (MSAA) to smooth the outer silhouettes of rotated polygons. OBS Studio, however, is optimized for performance and real-time compositing, meaning it typically renders the bounding geometry of sources using standard single-sample rasterization. As a result, boundary pixels are evaluated as either entirely inside or outside the polygon boundary, producing sharp, jagged edges along the perimeter.

High Contrast Exaggerates Edge Discrepancies

Aliasing is always present during non-orthogonal rasterization, but high-contrast graphics make it far more noticeable. When a bright element (such as pure white text, a bright border, or a vibrant HUD) meets a dark or transparent background, the luminance transition spans only a single pixel. The human eye is exceptionally sensitive to luminance contrast, making single-pixel stair-stepping immediately apparent.

Inadequate Texture Filtering Settings

By default, sources in OBS Studio may have their scale filtering set to Disable or Point, which applies nearest-neighbor sampling. When a texture undergoes non-uniform scaling or rotation, nearest-neighbor sampling maps transformed coordinates to the closest single texel, creating severe jaggedness both inside the graphic and along its alpha borders.

Even when set to Bilinear, filtering only blends a 2x2 grid of neighboring texels. Bilinear interpolation softens internal textures, but it struggles to cleanly resolve high-frequency details rotated across irregular angles.

Subpixel Alignment and Canvas Scaling

Rotating an asset shifts its coordinates into fractional, subpixel positions. If the canvas resolution does not match the base asset resolution, or if downscaling occurs between the base canvas and the stream output, these subpixel shifts are re-sampled. Re-sampling an already aliased edge during downscaling often introduces moiré patterns, flickering, and intensified pixel stepping.

How to Prevent Jagged Edges in OBS Studio

To eliminate or significantly reduce jagged rotation artifacts, apply the following adjustments:

  • Change the Scale Filtering Method: Right-click the affected source, navigate to Scale Filtering, and select Bicubic or Lanczos. These algorithms sample larger pixel grids (4x4 or sinc-based), significantly smoothing out transformation artifacts.
  • Add Transparent Padding in the Source File: Instead of letting OBS crop the graphic directly at its visible edge, export the graphic with 4 to 8 pixels of transparent padding around its borders. By feathering the outer edge slightly (anti-aliasing) inside image editing software like Photoshop or Illustrator before importing, the texture carries its own edge-smoothing data, bypassing the hard quad boundary of OBS.
  • Pre-Rotate the Asset: If the overlay is static, perform the rotation inside your graphic design software rather than using the OBS transformation matrix. Design software renders rotated vectors with full subpixel anti-aliasing.
  • Use Supersampling via Nested Scenes: Place the graphic into a dedicated scene at double its required size (e.g., 4K resolution). Rotate the graphic within that nested scene, then add the nested scene as a source into your main scene and scale it down. Scaling down an oversized source acts as Supersample Anti-Aliasing (SSAA), cleanly smoothing out jagged edges.
