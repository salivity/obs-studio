Syncing Wireless and SDI Video Feeds in OBS Studio

Combining wired SDI feeds with wireless video transmitters in OBS Studio often results in severe synchronization issues, primarily driven by latency disparities, clock drift, and audio misalignment. Wired SDI inputs deliver uncompressed video with virtually zero latency, whereas wireless transmitters introduce encoding, RF transmission, and decoding delays. This article examines the core technical challenges of mixing these two transmission mediums in a live production environment and outlines how to achieve seamless frame and audio synchronization within OBS Studio.

The Latency Gap Between SDI and Wireless Feeds

The primary hurdle in a hybrid setup is the intrinsic propagation delay of wireless systems compared to hardwired connections.

  • Wired SDI Feeds: Professional SDI connections transmit uncompressed digital video directly to a capture card (such as a Blackmagic DeckLink or AJA Corvid). The processing overhead is negligible, typically introducing less than one frame (roughly 10 to 30 milliseconds) of delay.
  • Wireless Transmitters: Wireless systems—even professional "zero-delay" units operating in the 5 GHz or 60 GHz bands (like Teradek Bolt or Hollyland Cosmo)—introduce a processing delay of roughly 1 millisecond up to several frames. Consumer or IP-based wireless transmitters (using H.264/H.265 compression over Wi-Fi) amplify this issue, introducing delays anywhere from 60 milliseconds to half a second due to encoding, packetization, and decoding stages.

When switching between a wired wide shot and a wireless roaming camera in OBS, this timing mismatch results in jarring visual cuts, spatial jump cuts, and noticeable desynchronization of fast-moving subjects across different camera angles.

Lack of Genlock and Frame Drift

Professional broadcast studios rely on genlock (generator locking) to synchronize the internal clocks of all cameras and capture devices to a single reference signal, ensuring every camera draws each frame at the exact same microsecond.

SDI environments frequently leverage genlock or frame-synchronizing capture cards. Wireless transmitters, however, break this reference loop. Even if the wireless receiver outputs an SDI signal to your capture card, the transmitter side is detached from the house sync. Consequently, the wireless feed will experience minor clock drift relative to the wired feeds. In OBS Studio, this manifests as periodic dropped or duplicated frames as the software attempts to conform disparate, free-running video clocks onto a single canvas frame rate (e.g., 60 fps).

Audio and Lip-Sync Mismatches

Because audio is typically captured via a dedicated mixing console connected directly via USB or line-in, it enters OBS with minimal latency, closely matching the wired SDI video.

When a presenter speaks on a wireless camera, their mouth movements will lag behind the audio because of the wireless transmitter's delay. Conversely, if audio is embedded directly into the wireless camera and passed to OBS via the receiver, cutting to a wired SDI camera will cause the video to appear ahead of the audio. Managing multiple cameras with distinct latency profiles forces producers to balance audio delays across every scene.

Solutions for Synchronizing Feeds in OBS Studio

To resolve these discrepancies and produce a cohesive multi-camera broadcast, several operational adjustments must be implemented within OBS Studio:

1. Measure the Frame Delta

Identify the slowest video source (typically the wireless camera). Point all cameras at a running digital millisecond timecode display (or use a clapperboard/flash test). Record the multi-view in OBS and inspect the recorded frames to determine the exact millisecond discrepancy between the wired SDI cameras and the wireless feed.

2. Apply the Render Delay Filter to SDI Inputs

Instead of trying to speed up the wireless feed—which is physically constrained by hardware—you must intentionally delay the faster SDI inputs to match the slowest feed:

  • Right-click the wired SDI source in the Sources panel.
  • Select Filters.
  • Under Effect Filters, add a Render Delay filter.
  • Enter the millisecond difference calculated during your latency test (OBS limits this filter to 500ms, which is sufficient for uncompressed and low-latency wireless systems).

3. Adjust Global Audio Sync Offsets

Once all video feeds are aligned to the same visual baseline, adjust the master audio source to match:

  • Open the Advanced Audio Properties via the gear icon in the Audio Mixer.
  • Add a Sync Offset (ms) to the incoming audio feed to bring it into alignment with the delayed video baseline.

4. Lock Refresh Rates and Disable Dynamic Buffering

Ensure all cameras, capture cards, and OBS canvas settings share identical frame rates (e.g., strictly 59.94 fps or 60 fps) to prevent variable frame pacing. In the properties of each capture device within OBS, set Buffering to Disable. Enabling buffering allows OBS to dynamically hold frames to prevent stutter, but this introduces variable, unpredictable latency that disrupts manual synchronization filters over long broadcasts.
