How OBS Studio Manages Audio FIFO to Stop Drift

During extended recording sessions lasting dozens of continuous hours, OBS Studio prevents audio and video desynchronization through a combination of nanosecond-precision timestamping, circular FIFO (first-in, first-out) buffering, and real-time dynamic resampling. Hardware clocks on independent devices inevitably drift, but OBS Studio continuously compares incoming audio frame timestamps against the master system clock, gently adjusting sample rates and manipulating the FIFO queue to ensure audio remains locked to video without audible artifacts.

The Source of Long-Term Audio Drift

Audio drift occurs because hardware oscillators are imperfect. An audio interface claiming a 48.0 kHz sample rate might actually sample at 47.998 kHz or 48.002 kHz. While an error of a few samples per second is imperceptible over a ten-minute recording, it compounds over twenty-four hours into seconds of latency, causing noticeable lipsync separation.

Monotonic Timestamps and Packet Ingestion

OBS Studio addresses hardware divergence at the ingestion layer. When an audio capture device provides raw audio packets, OBS immediately tags each packet with a Presentation Timestamp (PTS) derived from the operating system’s high-resolution monotonic clock (os_gettime_ns()).

Once timestamped, the audio data enters an internal FIFO ring buffer. Rather than relying on sample counts to determine playback timing, OBS relies strictly on these timestamps. This isolates the timing logic from the physical capture hardware's imperfect internal clock.

Dynamic Audio Resampling

The primary mechanism OBS Studio uses to prevent FIFO buffer overruns or underruns during long sessions is dynamic resampling. OBS monitors the rate at which audio samples arrive relative to how fast video frames are rendered:

  • Buffer Accumulation (Audio Arriving Too Fast): If the audio device's clock runs faster than the system clock, samples accumulate in the FIFO buffer. OBS compensates by dynamically adjusting the resampling ratio upward, consuming and compressing the extra samples into the expected timeframe.
  • Buffer Depletion (Audio Arriving Too Slow): If the capture device runs slow, the FIFO buffer drains toward empty. OBS slightly stretches the audio through interpolation, inserting calculated samples to maintain the stream without introducing silence or dropouts.

This dynamic resampling is handled via a fractional sinc resampler. Because the drift adjustments occur continuously in micro-increments (often mere fractions of a percent), the pitch shift and time-stretching remain entirely undetectable to the human ear.

Queue Clamping and Hard Sync Recovery

If an audio driver stalls, disconnects, or suffers an extreme spike in system latency, the FIFO queue can desynchronize beyond what dynamic resampling can smoothly correct. In these edge cases, OBS Studio utilizes threshold clamping:

  1. Audio Padding: If the time gap between the end of the last audio frame in the FIFO and the new incoming packet exceeds a specific threshold, OBS inserts silent samples matching the duration of the gap, realigning the stream to the current timeline.
  2. Audio Truncation: If delayed frames arrive that conflict with existing, newer timestamps, the stale data in the FIFO queue is discarded.

By prioritizing absolute monotonic time over raw sample continuity, OBS Studio ensures that no matter how long a recording runs, the audio FIFO queue neither exhausts system memory nor slips out of sync with the video renderer.
