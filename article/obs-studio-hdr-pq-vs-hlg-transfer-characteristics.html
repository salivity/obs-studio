OBS Studio HDR: PQ vs HLG Transfer Characteristics

Selecting between Perceptual Quantizer (PQ) and Hybrid Log-Gamma (HLG) in OBS Studio fundamentally alters how luminance, color volume, and dynamic range are encoded, previewed, and distributed in an HDR workflow. While both curves belong to the ITU-R BT.2100 standard and utilize the wide BT.2020 color gamut, they interpret light values in drastically different ways. This choice determines your capture compatibility, downstream encoding requirements, tonemapping behavior for SDR outputs, and viewer display accuracy on platforms like YouTube.

Absolute vs. Relative Luminance

The core technical difference between PQ and HLG lies in how they define brightness values:

  • PQ (ST 2084): PQ uses an absolute luminance scale targeting up to 10,000 cd/m² (nits). A specific digital signal value in OBS corresponds to an exact nit value on an HDR display. If a game highlights an element at 1,000 nits, a PQ-configured stream explicitly targets that exact luminance.
  • HLG: HLG uses a relative, scene-referred curve that adapts dynamically to the peak brightness of the viewer's display. It does not dictate absolute nits; instead, the dynamic range scales up or down based on the capabilities of the rendering hardware and ambient room lighting.

Source Matching and Capture Integrity

Your selection in OBS Studio under Settings > Advanced > Video > Color Space (Rec. 2100 (PQ) or Rec. 2100 (HLG)) must align with your capture sources to prevent washed-out colors or severe clipping:

  • PC and Console Gaming: Modern gaming systems (Windows 11 Auto HDR, Xbox Series X/S, PlayStation 5) and capture cards almost universally output standard HDR10, which relies on the PQ transfer curve. Selecting PQ in OBS preserves this signal 1:1 without forcing an internal transfer function conversion.
  • Broadcast Cameras and Live Production: Many professional mirrorless and cinema cameras output HLG natively via HDMI or SDI to avoid complex HDR color grading during live acquisition. If your primary source is a broadcast camera shooting in HLG, setting your canvas to Rec. 2100 (HLG) maintains proper luminance levels without manual lutting or tonemapping.

Ingestion, Tonemapping, and SDR Preview

OBS Studio must handle compositing and previewing HDR content locally, which introduces specific challenges:

  • SDR Tonemapping: If you record or stream an SDR fallback alongside your HDR feed, OBS must tonemap the wide dynamic range down to Rec. 709. Because PQ operates on absolute values with high peak targets, poorly managed PQ tonemapping can cause midtones to look dim or blow out highlights. HLG naturally transitions to SDR more gracefully because its lower curve mirrors standard gamma curves (Rec. 709) up to approximately 50% signal level.
  • Overlays and Color Mixing: Standard SDR overlays, browser sources, and alerts must be tonemapped into the HDR canvas. With PQ, OBS uses the "SDR White Level" setting (typically 100–300 nits) to position SDR assets within the absolute 10,000-nit space. With HLG, overlays are assigned a relative percentage of total output, making asset brightness less predictable across different displays.

Platform Support and Streaming Encoders

Delivery platforms dictate which transfer function yields the best viewer experience:

  • YouTube Live HDR: YouTube supports both PQ and HLG over RTMP (via HEVC) and HLS (via HEVC or AV1). However, PQ requires static HDR10 metadata (Mastering Display Color Volume and Content Light Level) to be recognized and processed properly by consumer TVs. If metadata is stripped or misconfigured, PQ video can render incorrectly.
  • HLG Resilience: HLG does not require metadata to display an HDR image. If transmitted over a system that drops metadata, HLG streams will still be decoded by compatible displays and gracefully displayed on SDR panels without severe color degradation.

Summary: Which Should You Choose?

  • Choose Rec. 2100 (PQ) if you stream or record HDR gameplay from PC or consoles, master fixed-luminance content, and target platforms where HDR10 playback fidelity is the priority.
  • Choose Rec. 2100 (HLG) if your workflow centers on live camera feeds, requires robust fallback on legacy SDR displays without complex tonemapping, or relies on transmission pipelines that strip HDR metadata.
