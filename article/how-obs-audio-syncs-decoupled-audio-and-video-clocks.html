How OBS Audio Syncs Decoupled Audio and Video Clocks

This article examines the internal architecture of the OBS Studio audio engine (obs-audio) and its mechanisms for resolving clock drift between decoupled audio and video inputs. Readers will learn how the engine captures monotonic hardware timestamps, buffers asynchronous data streams, performs dynamic resampling, and synchronizes audio samples with video rendering frames to guarantee drift-free multiplexing and streaming.

The Problem of Decoupled Hardware Clocks

In a typical production environment, audio interfaces, capture cards, and webcams rely on independent physical crystal oscillators. A 48 kHz audio interface might run at an actual 48,001 Hz, while a video capture device running at a nominal 60 fps may deliver frames slightly faster or slower. Because these devices do not share a common word clock or genlock, their data streams inherently drift over time. Without an active reconciliation mechanism, this divergence causes noticeable audio-video synchronization errors (lip-sync drift), buffer underruns, or buffer overflows.

Ingestion and Monotonic Timestamping

The obs-audio engine neutralizes hardware-level timing discrepancies at the point of capture. When an audio packet arrives from an input driver (such as WASAPI, CoreAudio, or ALSA), OBS immediately stamps the buffer with a high-resolution timestamp using the system's monotonic clock (os_gettime_ns).

Crucially, the engine does not trust the nominal hardware sample count alone to calculate elapsed time over long durations. Instead, it measures the rate at which samples are delivered relative to the system monotonic clock. This anchors the audio stream to the same timeline used by the OBS video rendering thread.

The Audio Ring Buffer and Frame Alignment

Captured audio chunks pass into circular ring buffers maintained by the audio-io subsystem. The video engine ticks at discrete frame intervals dictated by the configured project framerate (for example, every 16.66 ms for 60 fps). During each video render tick, the audio engine computes the exact time window represented by the current video frame.

The audio engine processes samples in discrete blocks—typically 1,024 samples per mix buffer. It uses the monotonic timestamps to slice and advance audio buffers, aligning incoming sample boundaries with the corresponding video frame's Presentation Timestamp (PTS).

Drift Compensation and Resampling

When the audio capture clock deviates from the system clock, the audio buffer will either fill faster than the rendering pipeline consumes it (clock too fast) or run dry (clock too slow). The obs-audio engine continuously monitors the offset between the audio arrival timestamps and the render timeline.

To correct divergence without causing audible pops, clicks, or pitch shifts:

  1. Timestamp Adjustment: Minor phase jitters within normal operating tolerances are absorbed by the internal ring buffer margins.
  2. Resampling: If a sustained drift rate is detected between the source's native clock and the target timeline, OBS passes the stream through an internal resampler (often backed by libsamplerate or speexdsp). The engine dynamically modulates the conversion ratio by minute fractions, imperceptibly stretching or compressing the audio samples to keep the buffer centered.
  3. Buffer Clamping: In extreme cases where hardware stalls or disconnects cause an unrecoverable gap, the engine drops excess samples or injects silence to realign the stream to the current timeline rather than allowing persistent desynchronization.

Downstream Mixing and Muxer Handoff

Once synchronized, multiple audio inputs are mixed into the configured output tracks. The mixed audio blocks are tagged with final presentation timestamps synchronized precisely to the video frame presentation timestamps. When passed to the encoding subsystem (such as FFmpeg or native hardware encoders), both elementary streams share a unified timeline. The container muxer (such as MP4, MKV, or FLV) can then interleave the audio and video packets with matching timecodes, ensuring that client media players decode and present both tracks in perfect synchronization.
