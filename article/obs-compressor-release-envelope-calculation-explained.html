OBS Compressor Release Envelope Calculation Explained

This article explores how OBS Studio calculates the release envelope in its native compressor filter when processing sharp percussive transients. It covers the underlying mathematical modeling, the peak detection mechanism, the exponential decay function, and the practical acoustic impact of these calculations on short, high-amplitude audio spikes.

Peak Detection and Level Tracking

OBS Studio relies on a peak-detecting dynamics algorithm within its native audio filters (compressor-filter.c). When a signal enters the compressor, the engine continuously calculates the absolute amplitude of each sample frame. Percussive transients—such as snare hits, microphone pops, or mechanical keyboard clicks—feature near-instantaneous rise times and immediate drop-offs. The compressor identifies whether this detected peak exceeds the user-configured threshold in the decibel domain.

Envelope Smoothing Formula

Dynamic range control requires smoothing to prevent abrupt gain changes from creating square-wave harmonic distortion. OBS Studio achieves this using a discrete-time, single-pole Infinite Impulse Response (IIR) low-pass filter to model the envelope state.

The time constant for the release phase is derived using the sample rate (\(f_s\)) and the user-defined release time in milliseconds (\(t_{\text{rel}}\)):

\[\alpha_{\text{rel}} = \exp\left(-\frac{1}{t_{\text{rel}} \cdot 0.001 \cdot f_s}\right)\]

This smoothing coefficient (\(\alpha_{\text{rel}}\)) defines the rate of decay per audio sample.

State Transition During Sharp Transients

When a sharp transient passes through the compressor:

  1. Attack Phase: The signal rapidly exceeds the threshold, switching the detector into the attack state. The envelope quickly tracks up to the peak gain reduction level.
  2. Transition Threshold: Because a percussive transient decays within milliseconds, the incoming sample amplitude drops below the currently stored envelope state almost immediately.
  3. Release Phase Trigger: The filter switches to the release calculation loop as soon as the calculated target gain reduction is less than the current filter state.

During each subsequent sample interval (\(n\)), the compressor updates its gain reduction envelope using the following recurrence relation:

\[\text{Envelope}[n] = \alpha_{\text{rel}} \cdot \text{Envelope}[n - 1] + (1 - \alpha_{\text{rel}}) \cdot \text{TargetGain}[n]\]

Logarithmic vs. Linear Decay Dynamics

Because the release coefficient is applied exponentially, the gain recovery follows an analog-style asymptotic curve. In practice:

  • Initial Recovery: Immediately following the percussive peak, the delta between the compressed level and unity gain is at its maximum. Consequently, the rate of gain release (measured in dB/sec) is highest right after the transient peak exits.
  • Tail Recovery: As the compressor's envelope approaches unity gain (0 dB of reduction), the release rate decelerates logarithmically.

Processing Behavior on Percussive Material

The interaction between percussive spikes and this exponential release envelope generates two distinct conditions:

  • Fast Release Settings (< 50 ms): Gain recovers before the body of the drum or voice transient finishes vibrating. While this preserves room ambiance and loudness, it can cause intermodulation distortion or rapid "pumping," as the tail of the transient is boosted unnaturally relative to the initial strike.
  • Slow Release Settings (> 100 ms): The compressor envelope clamps down on the transient and remains suppressed long after the transient has finished. This suppresses subsequent low-level speech or delicate audio events that occur immediately after the strike.

By relying on exponential time-constant decay rather than a linear slope, OBS Studio minimizes audible clicks during state transitions, ensuring that short, severe amplitude spikes are smoothed out naturally over the duration of the selected release interval.
